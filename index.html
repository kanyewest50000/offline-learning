The problem

"Just put it online" is not a strategy for a third of humanity.

EdTech quietly assumes broadband. Where that assumption fails, learning platforms fail with it — silently, and at enormous scale.

  • 2.6 Bpeople have no meaningful internet access, concentrated in rural and low-income regions.
  • 463 Mstudents couldn't be reached by remote learning during recent school closures — most because of connectivity, not devices.
  • $8–14 / GBeffective mobile data cost in the least-connected markets, versus pennies in wealthy ones. Streaming video lectures is economically absurd.
  • 6–20 hrs / wktypical grid electricity availability in many rural schools we serve. Everything we ship is built for intermittent power.
How it works

Pack. Deploy. Sync — whenever the network shows up.

The entire platform is designed around one rule: the network is a bonus, never a dependency.

1

Pack the curriculum

Educators assemble Content Packs — versioned, compressed bundles of lessons, video, exercises, and assessments. A full K-12 year in 3 languages fits in under 48 GB. Packs are signed, deduplicated at the block level, and diffable, so an update ships only what changed.

2

Deploy to the edge

A Hearth Hub — our solar-tolerant classroom server — broadcasts the pack over local Wi-Fi to any device: donated tablets, teachers' phones, or our e-ink readers. No accounts, no app stores, no SIM cards. A classroom is live 20 minutes after unboxing.

3

Sync opportunistically

Progress, assessments, and teacher notes queue locally. When any device in the mesh touches connectivity — a teacher visiting town, a bus with a hub driving past — the whole classroom's data rides along, encrypted, in kilobytes. We call it sneakernet with manners.

Technical brief

The boring, load-bearing details.

Offline-first is not a feature flag. It's a different set of physics. Here is how we handle them.

Delta sync protocol

Packs are content-addressed Merkle DAGs. A device advertises its root hashes; peers respond with only missing blocks. A typical monthly curriculum update transfers ~140 MB instead of re-shipping 48 GB. Transfers resume mid-block after power loss.

Conflict resolution

Learner progress is modeled as CRDTs (grow-only sets for completions, LWW-registers for preferences, PN-counters for attempt tallies), so weeks-divergent devices merge deterministically with no server arbitration and no lost work.

Opportunistic transport

The sync daemon treats every channel as a courier: Wi-Fi Direct, hub-to-hub 900 MHz radio, USB drives (yes, really — signed and verified), and finally the internet if it appears. Priority queues put assessment results ahead of telemetry, always.

Storage honesty

Flash on cheap devices lies about writes. We journal everything, checksum every block on read, and keep 2 rotating pack generations so a corrupt update can never brick a classroom mid-semester.

FAQ

Questions we actually get asked.

Does "offline" mean the content gets stale?

No. Packs are versioned and delta-synced: when any device in a mesh touches connectivity, only changed blocks propagate outward — typically ~140 MB for a monthly full-curriculum update. A district's most remote classroom is usually current within two weeks of an update, with no one doing anything on purpose.

What devices do learners need?

Anything with a browser: donated tablets, shared family phones, school desktops. The hub serves everything over local Wi-Fi. For device-poor classrooms we supply e-ink readers with 3-week battery life at cost.

How do certifications work without a server to verify them?

Assessment results are hashed and timestamped on-device against the hub's secure clock, then countersigned when they eventually reach the regional sync server. The certificate is verifiable from the paper printout alone — a QR code contains the signed proof.

Who owns the learner data?

The school, then the ministry — never us. Data is pseudonymous by default, stays on regional infrastructure, and every hub has a one-button full export in open formats. Our contracts forbid us from monetizing learner data, and our architecture makes it mostly impossible anyway.

What happens when a hub breaks 400 km from a technician?

Nine parts are field-replaceable with the included screwdriver, guided by an illustrated repair booklet (and an offline video series on the hub itself — which mirrors to neighboring hubs, so the repair guide survives the failure it documents). Median field repair: 11 minutes.

Are you open source?

The sync protocol, pack format, and hardware schematics are published. The adaptive-learning engine is source-available for ministries and auditors. We think infrastructure for public education should be inspectable by the public that depends on it.
